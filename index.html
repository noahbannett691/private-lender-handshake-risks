The Risks Private Lenders Miss in a Handshake Deal

Most private loans that go bad do not fail because the property was worthless or the borrower was a fraud. They fail in the ordinary ways: a payment stops, a story changes, and the lender reaches for documents that were never signed. The handshake deal feels efficient at the start because it skips exactly the steps that protect the lender at the end. Seasoned Private Mortgage Lawyers can usually predict how a handshake loan ends from the list of things the lender decided not to bother with.

Nothing registered means nothing secured

The most common gap is registration. A lender who takes a signed note but never registers a mortgage against the property is, in law, mostly an unsecured creditor with a story. If the borrower sells the property, borrows against it from someone else, or is pursued by other creditors, the handshake lender stands in line behind everyone who did register. The property that was supposed to be the security was never legally tied to the loan.

The borrower's other debts are invisible

Handshake lenders rarely search title, so they rarely know what they are lending behind. A property that looks debt-free over coffee can carry a first mortgage, tax arrears and a lien or two on the register. Priority is everything in a default, and a lender who never looked does not know their position until it is too late to change it. By then, the equity they were counting on belongs to the creditors ahead of them.

Verbal terms change with memory

Interest, maturity, default consequences: on a handshake, each side remembers the version that suits them. When relations are good the difference does not matter; when a payment is missed it becomes the whole dispute. A written mortgage does not just record the deal, it removes the argument about what the deal was. Courts enforce documents, not recollections, and the side with the paper usually wins the argument the other side started.

Enforcement becomes a lawsuit instead of a process

A registered mortgage carries defined remedies, including the power of sale process with its notices and timelines. An unsecured handshake loan carries one remedy: sue, wait, and hope the borrower still has something when judgment arrives. The time and cost difference between the two routes is measured in years and tens of thousands of dollars, against a borrower who has already shown they cannot or will not pay.

Family and friendship raise the stakes, not lower them

Many handshake loans happen between people who know each other, which is exactly why the paperwork feels awkward. But relationships are what defaults destroy. A properly documented loan between friends survives a late payment, because the terms were settled while everyone was calm. An undocumented one turns a money problem into a personal betrayal, and usually ends both the loan and the friendship.

The fix costs less than one missed payment

Everything the handshake skips, a title search, a registered mortgage, written terms, insurance, is a routine, modestly priced package arranged before funding. Compared with the value of the loan itself, the protection is a rounding error. Private lending is a legitimate, useful business when it is documented like one. The lenders who treat it that way are the ones still lending, profitably, years later.

The short version

The risks in a handshake deal are not exotic: nothing registered, no title search, no written terms, no real remedy, and a relationship on the line. Each one is removed by the standard documentation a private lending lawyer prepares before money moves. The handshake is not a shortcut; it is the risk itself.
